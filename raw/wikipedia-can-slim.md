## Overview

CAN SLIM is a growth stock investment methodology developed by William O'Neil (founder of Investor's Business Daily) that identifies seven characteristics of top-performing stocks before major price advances.

## The acronym

### C — Current Quarterly Earnings
Current earnings should be up at least **25%** in the most recent financial quarter vs the same quarter the previous year. Accelerating earnings across recent quarters signals positive momentum.

### A — Annual Earnings Growth
Annual earnings growth must increase **25% or more over three years**, with annual returns on equity reaching **17% minimum**.

### N — New Product or Service
Companies require continuous innovation and development — enabling stocks to break from consolidation patterns and achieve higher valuations.

### S — Supply and Demand
Stock demand is gauged through trading volume, particularly during price increases.

### L — Leader or Laggard
O'Neil advises buying "the leading stock in a leading industry." The relative price strength rating measures 12-month stock performance against the S&P 500 or comparable indices.

### I — Institutional Sponsorship
Ownership by mutual funds, banks, and institutions in recent quarters is favorable. The accumulation/distribution rating quantifies institutional activity.

### M — Market Direction
Three categories: confirmed uptrend, uptrend under pressure, correction. O'Neil prefers investing during definite uptrends, since "three out of four stocks tend to follow the general market direction."

## Strategy mechanics

- Combines technical and fundamental analysis.
- Seeks stocks emerging from "cup-with-handle" consolidation patterns lasting minimum **seven weeks**.
- Strict stop-loss discipline: no more than **7–8% below entry points**.

## Recognition & performance

- The American Association of Individual Investors named CAN SLIM the top-performing strategy from 1998–2009.
- In 2015, the Innovator IBD 50 ETF launched using these criteria.
- Criticism: O'Neil's personal mutual fund ventures produced "lackluster returns," suggesting implementation challenges beyond the theoretical framework.
